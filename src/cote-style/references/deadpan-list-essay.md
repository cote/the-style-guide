# Deadpan list essay

A voice mode for pieces that catalogue an absurd present or near-future.
Reach for this when the topic is itself ridiculous and naming the
ridiculousness would weaken it.

## The core move

Describe the absurd scene literally. Do not point at the joke. The
reader does the work.

Bad (analytical commentary on the absurd):

> The Jevons read is that we'll find more work to do with the freed capacity. Probably true at the industry level. That layer was always a little suspect.

Good (literal scene that is itself the commentary):

> An executive sits down in front of an AI chat app and says: how much ground beef should we order for next quarter? How much steel? How many people should staff the night shift? Should we open that new branch in Cleveland?

The deadpan description of the executive talking to a chat window is
doing more work than any framework name. The joke is the scene.

## Running gag as connective tissue

Pick one absurd primitive and let it recur across sections without
commentary. In an AI-displacement piece, the primitive might be "the
executive sits at a chat app and asks for X." Show it in Decks,
Measuring, Comms, Code, New Features, Planning. Each appearance is a
different domain; the consistency is the bit.

Do not introduce the gag. Do not retire it. Do not lampshade it. Just
use it.

## What to cut

When polishing in this mode, scan for and delete:

- Framework-naming as analysis ("the Jevons paradox of X", "the ZIRP-cover hypothesis", "this is really about Y"). Naming the framework breaks the spell.
- Reflective summary paragraphs at the end. ("What this list has in common is..." can survive if it stays descriptive; anything evaluative gets cut.)
- "This layer was always a little suspect" / "the open question is" sentences. The piece is not asking a question. It is showing a scene.
- Industry data points used to justify the claim. The claim is being shown, not argued.

## What to keep that looks like hedging but is not

Speculative hedging is part of the voice. Keep:

- "Maybe X" / "maybe Y" stacks.
- "I don't know, maybe that's what people are doing" mid-paragraph.
- "Of course, X is often hard, so maybe Y."
- "I think you still need humans to do Z."
- "This feels like where most of it lands. Maybe?"

The distinction: speculative hedging *about what is happening or why* is
voice. Analytical framing *that names the thing as a known pattern* is
the commentary to cut.

## What to keep

- Concrete jobs and concrete tasks. Ground beef, steel, opening a branch in Cleveland, M&A boilerplate, PDF routing. The more boring and real the example, the funnier the AI replacement.
- The mild parenthetical aside, if it adds to the scene rather than commenting on it. "(checks notes)" is fine. "(which is to say, this is the Jevons paradox)" is not.
- Direct quotes from real sources, when the actual words are already deadpan.
- One-line section endings that just stop. "Have the AI do it. Have a person review it." End of section. No closer.

## Structure

- Many short sections under H2 headers. Each names a domain.
- Each section is one to three short paragraphs.
- Lead with the literal scene. Optional second paragraph adds a concrete example. Stop.
- Open the whole piece with the question or framing, then "Here's where I land." No thesis paragraph.
- Close with a one-line invitation or a small concrete observation. "What have you come across?" Not a summary.

## Sentence register

- Mostly declarative. Subject, verb, thing.
- Short fragments are fine. "Tireless. Independent. Available." (Especially after a long sentence.)
- Light profanity is fine, sparing. The deadpan is doing the heavy lifting, so the swearing is seasoning, not punchline.
- No em-dashes. Hyphens with spaces.
- Straight quotes only.

## What this style is NOT

- Not a rant. Rants are emotional. This is dry.
- Not satire that exaggerates. The whole point is that the literal description is already absurd; no exaggeration needed.
- Not a thinkpiece with a thesis to defend. There is no claim being argued. There is a tour being given.
- Not a list of takes. A take is "AI layoffs are mostly cover for over-hiring." This style would instead describe the scene of the CEO asking the AI for cover language, and let the reader draw the conclusion.

## Trigger

Reach for this style when the brief is:

- "Drippingly ironic"
- "Void of commentary"
- "Let it stand on its own"
- "Sass, but don't explain it"
- Or when the topic is itself the joke (AI doing X, executives doing Y, the corporate world being weird at itself).
