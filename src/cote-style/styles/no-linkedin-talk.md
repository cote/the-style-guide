# No LinkedIn-talk

A negative / edit-pass style. Load this when the user asks you to
strip LinkedIn-clichés from a draft, not when they're asking you to
write in that voice.

Typical triggers:

- "Make sure this doesn't read like LinkedIn."
- "Remove any LinkedIn-talk."
- "De-LinkedIn this draft."
- "Cut the LinkedIn cliches."
- "This sounds too LinkedIn - fix it."

Unlike a prose-voice style, this one produces no new voice on its
own. It's an edit pass that surfaces and rewrites the recognizable
LinkedIn pattern set. Pair it with whatever positive style is actually
in play (Coté, the user's own, whatever).

## The two diagnostic tests

Before scanning phrase lists, run these two tests on the draft:

1. **First-two-lines swap test.** Could you swap the first two lines
   of the draft with the first two lines of a hundred other LinkedIn
   posts and have anyone notice? If yes, the opening is doing pattern
   work, not content work. Rewrite until the opening is unmistakably
   about *this* piece.
2. **Delete-the-setup-sentences test.** Cross out every "setup"
   sentence (any sentence whose job is to signal "important thing
   coming"). Re-read what's left. If the piece still carries meaning,
   the setups were decoration - remove them.

These two tests catch more LinkedIn-talk than any phrase list.

## The core structural template to break

Most LinkedIn cliché-heavy posts follow a four-part shape:

1. Dramatic opening (attention grab, often a "hot take" or a "here's
   the truth" pattern).
2. Short personal narrative (usually 2-4 sentences).
3. A bold "lesson" statement, often italicized or on its own line.
4. A closing line engineered for engagement (rhetorical question,
   "agree? disagree?", invitation to comment).

If the draft fits this shape, that's the pattern to break. Not every
part has to go, but if all four are present as a template, the piece
reads as pre-fabricated even when the story is real.

Rewrite by replacing the template with three actual pieces of
content: (a) the specific situation, (b) the decision the writer
made, (c) the result they observed. Those three specifics beat the
four-part template every time.

## Phrase lists to strike

Scan for these. Any hit is a flag. Rewrite inline; don't leave the
flag as a comment.

### The "I'm profound" openers

Sentences whose job is to announce depth before any depth arrives.

- "Here's the truth..."
- "Let that sink in."
- "Let's unpack this."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "If you know, you know."
- "I don't usually post things like this, but..."
- "I'm about to say something controversial..."
- "This might shock you, but..."
- "It's not what you think."
- "Read that again."
- "Buckle up."
- "Here's the harsh reality."
- "People buy from people."
- "It is what it is."

**Fix:** Delete the opener. Start with the specific claim or scene.
If the point is strong, it lands without stage directions.

### Corporate-inspirational slogans

Phrases so broad they're impossible to disagree with - and therefore
useless.

- "Leadership is not a title."
- "Your network is your net worth."
- "Culture eats strategy for breakfast."
- "People don't leave companies; they leave managers."
- "Done is better than perfect."
- "We rise by lifting others."
- "Be the best version of yourself."
- "Step out of your comfort zone."
- "This moment taught me that..."
- "Work smarter, not harder."
- "Unleash your potential."

**Fix:** Replace with the specific situation. "This moment taught me
X" → "When [specific event] happened, I changed how I [specific
action]. Now [specific result]."

### "Deep life lesson" claims

Openers that promise wisdom then deliver a conclusion too broad to
test.

- "I learned this the hard way."
- "What no one tells you about success is..."
- "Nobody is coming to save you."
- "Stay humble. Stay hungry."
- "Consistency is the real flex."
- "Trust the process."
- "Your habits determine your future."
- "The game has changed."

**Fix:** If there's a real lesson, walk the reader through it:
what was tried, what failed, what changed, what happened next. Ditch
the maxim.

### Humble-brag structures

- "I'm happy to announce..."
- "I'm humbled and excited..."
- "Never thought I'd be sharing this..."
- "Quick update:"
- "So this happened today..."
- "Full circle moment."

**Fix:** Just say the news. "I got X" beats "I'm humbled and excited
to share that I got X." The audience does not need emotional
packaging.

### Hot-take intros

Openers whose job is to manufacture tension before the argument
appears.

- "Unpopular opinion:"
- "No one is talking about this enough."
- "This might upset some people..."
- "Not sure who needs to hear this, but..."

**Fix:** State the claim directly. If it's actually contested, the
reader will feel that from the substance. If it's not actually
contested, dropping the warning label reveals that.

### Newer additions (spreading fast)

- "Let me be very clear."
- "This changed everything for me."
- "Read this before you post today."
- "I wish someone told me this earlier."
- "Soft skills are the new hard skills."
- "This is your sign."

### AI-prompted LinkedIn tells (Mollick's short-mute list)

These are the phrases that mark a post as poorly-prompted AI, not
just a human LinkedIn cliché:

- "doing the heavy lifting"
- "the real question is"
- "here's the thing nobody is talking about"
- "that's the real story"
- "what most people miss"
- "this is where it gets interesting"
- "it's not about X, it's about Y"

**Fix:** The last one is especially over-used - the "not X, but Y"
sentence shape (see also `references/ai-writing-tells.md`). Rewrite
as a direct claim.

## Structural tells beyond phrases

Even without the exact phrases above, watch for these shapes:

### The one-line paragraph cascade

LinkedIn-native writing often puts every sentence on its own line
with a blank line between. That's a platform-formatting habit, not a
prose style. Read the piece as if it were a paragraph. If the
sentences don't actually earn their own paragraph break, collapse
them.

### The engagement-baiting closer

- "Agree? Disagree?"
- "What's your take?"
- "Tag someone who needs to hear this."
- "Comment below."
- "Repost if you found this valuable."
- Any rhetorical question whose obvious job is to farm comments.

**Fix:** End on a real thought. If a question closes the piece, it
should be one the writer genuinely wants an answer to, not one whose
job is to trigger replies.

### The italicized "lesson" line

A short, sometimes-italicized sentence on its own line, stating "the"
lesson. Often accompanied by three-dots-on-a-line or an emoji.

**Fix:** Fold the lesson into the piece. If it's the piece's actual
point, it should appear as prose, not as a bumper-sticker.

### The emoji bullet list

Bullet points led by a checkmark ✅, sparkles ✨, arrow ➡, or fire
🔥 emoji.

**Fix:** Plain bullets. Or, better, prose. If the content is truly
list-shaped, the reader doesn't need visual candy to notice.

### Excessive #hashtags in the body

More than about three hashtags total, or hashtags mid-sentence
("thinking about #platformengineering and #DevOps today").

**Fix:** Move to a single hashtag block at the end, or cut entirely
if the platform doesn't require them.

## What to keep

Not every phrase common to LinkedIn is a cliché. Some are just normal
English:

- "Here's what we found." (fine - it's specific and followed by
  content)
- "I've been thinking about X." (fine - genuine framing)
- "This week I [did specific thing]." (fine - specific news)

The target is the *dramatic setup lines* that try to signal insight,
humility, or bravery without doing any actual work. Replace them
with: **the specific situation, the decision made, the result
observed.** Those three details create a voice that no template can
duplicate.

## Self-edit checklist

Before handing the edit back:

- [ ] First two lines could not be swapped with a hundred other
      LinkedIn posts.
- [ ] No phrases from any list above (or a very specific justification
      for one that stayed).
- [ ] No "not X, it's Y" as sentence shape.
- [ ] No "here's the thing" / "let me be very clear" / "let that
      sink in" / other stage directions.
- [ ] No four-part template (dramatic opener → narrative → lesson →
      engagement closer). At most two of those parts.
- [ ] No italicized single-line "lesson" bumper-sticker.
- [ ] No engagement-baiting closer. Close on a real thought.
- [ ] No checkmark / sparkle / arrow / fire emoji bullets.
- [ ] Every abstract claim replaced with (a) specific situation,
      (b) specific decision, (c) specific result - or cut.
