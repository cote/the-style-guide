# Tyler Cowen style questions

A question-generation style modeled on Tyler Cowen's interviewing on
*Conversations with Tyler*. Use when the user asks for "Tyler Cowen
style questions", interview prep in his mode, or a set of prompts /
question outlines that lean curious, wide-ranging, and unexpectedly
personal.

## What this style is for

Generating interview questions, conversation prompts, or research
questions for a specific person. The output is a numbered or bulleted
list of questions - not prose, not an interview transcript. The user
will run the actual conversation; this style just produces the
questions they'd ask.

Typical asks that should trigger this style:

- "Give me Tyler Cowen style questions for [guest]."
- "Do interview prep for [guest] in the Cowen style."
- "What would Tyler Cowen ask [guest]?"
- "Podcast questions for [guest], Cowen-ish."

## The core moves

Cowen's interview style is recognizable because he does a few things
almost every episode. Any set of questions in his style should hit
most of these:

1. **Read everything.** The premise is that the interviewer has read
   the guest's books, papers, obscure blog posts, dissertation, and
   any interviews they've done elsewhere. Questions cite specific
   passages, arguments, and side remarks by name. Never generic ("tell
   me about your work on X") - always specific ("in chapter 4 you
   argue Y - but how does that square with Z from your 2019 paper?").
2. **Wide-ranging, cross-domain.** Jump from the guest's core
   expertise to an adjacent field they've written a paragraph about,
   to their personal biography, to a food or music question, back to
   their expertise. The juxtaposition is the point. Don't group by
   theme; let the topics collide.
3. **Personal / biographical.** Where did they grow up? What was
   their childhood diet? What did their parents do? What was the
   formative experience? Cultural tastes, favorite obscure films,
   cuisines, cities. This is not fluff - it's the material that makes
   the guest a full person and often reveals the actual origin of
   their intellectual habits.
4. **"Production function" framing.** A signature move: "What is
   your production function?" / "What is the [X] production function
   for [Y]?" - a request for the guest's underlying model of how they
   generate output, how a field advances, how a country produces
   scientists, how a genre produces classics. Use this framing
   sparingly, one or two per set.
5. **Overrated / underrated.** A quickfire segment: name a person,
   idea, city, book, cuisine, and ask if it's overrated or underrated.
   The list should be tuned to the guest - things they'd have a real
   view on. Not "overrated: pizza" for a physicist; "overrated: string
   theory" for a physicist.
6. **Direct, sometimes uncomfortably so.** Ask the question the guest
   has been dodging in other interviews. Ask about failures, dead
   ends, unpopular views, things they've changed their mind on.
   Never gotcha - always genuinely curious - but never softball
   either.
7. **Comparative / cross-country.** "Why does X work differently in
   [country A] than in [country B]?" "What does [culture] get right
   about Y that [other culture] gets wrong?" Works best when the
   guest has serious exposure to more than one country.
8. **The "steelman" and the "why is this wrong" pair.** Ask the guest
   to steelman a view they oppose. Also ask them why a view they hold
   might be wrong. Both reveal more than direct advocacy questions.
9. **Ask about tacit knowledge.** "What do you know how to do that
   you can't teach?" "What do people in your field learn that never
   makes it into the textbooks?" "What's the thing your students
   don't get until year five?"
10. **Concrete over abstract.** "What is your favorite [specific
    obscure thing]?" beats "What kind of [broad category] do you
    like?" Specificity forces a real answer.

## Question shape

- **First-person, direct.** "I've been wondering..." / "What's your
  view on..." / "Why do you think..." / "How would you..." Not
  "Some might argue..." throat-clearing.
- **One question per question.** Don't stack three sub-questions in
  one bullet. The guest can't hold them.
- **Short.** Two sentences max, usually one. The setup is a specific
  fact or citation; the question is a sharp ask.
- **Cite specifics.** If a question references the guest's own work,
  name the book / paper / essay by title (or year, or a distinctive
  phrase from it). "In [Title], you say X. Do you still believe that?"
- **Assume expertise.** Don't explain concepts to the guest. Don't
  contextualize a term they invented. If a question needs a term the
  audience won't know, that's the guest's problem to unpack, not the
  interviewer's.
- **No preamble.** Don't warm up the guest with softballs. First
  question should be substantive and specific.

## Structure of a good question set

A typical Cowen-style prep sheet for a 60-90 minute conversation:

1. **Opening** - one specific, substantive question. No throat-clearing.
   Something the guest hasn't been asked before.
2. **Core expertise** - 6-10 questions that engage seriously with the
   guest's work. Cite specific arguments, chapters, papers. Include at
   least one steelman and at least one "why might you be wrong" prompt.
3. **Cross-domain jumps** - 3-5 questions from adjacent or unrelated
   fields where the guest has a view. Economics-of-X, sociology-of-Y,
   history-of-Z that isn't their day job but they've thought about.
4. **Production function segment** - one or two "what is your model of
   how X gets produced" questions, tuned to their field.
5. **Personal / biographical** - 4-6 questions about upbringing,
   formative experiences, family, cultural tastes, cities they've
   lived in, food, music, obscure influences. Not fluff - the through
   line often shows here.
6. **Overrated / underrated** - one segment, 8-15 rapid items tuned to
   the guest.
7. **Tacit knowledge** - one or two "what do you know that you can't
   teach" questions.
8. **Close** - one meta or reflective question. Not "what's next for
   you" (that's a PR question). Something like "what's a question
   you wish more interviewers asked you?" or "what have you changed
   your mind about in the last five years?"

Adjust proportions based on the length of the conversation and the
guest's temperament, but always include personal / biographical and
overrated / underrated - those are load-bearing.

## Word choice

### Use freely

- Specific proper nouns. Names of the guest's books, papers, essays,
  favorite writers, cities they've lived in, disciplines they've
  crossed. The specificity is the signal that the interviewer has
  actually done the work.
- Cross-domain framings: "production function", "steelman", "tacit
  knowledge", "underrated / overrated", "revealed preference",
  "comparative advantage."
- Cowen-signature phrases: "What is your model of X?", "What would
  it take to change your mind?", "What is the [X] production
  function?", "Why don't more people believe Y?", "What is the case
  for Z that you find least stupid?"

### Never use

- Generic openers: "Tell us about your journey", "What inspired you
  to...", "Walk us through..."
- Softball praise-baiting: "You've had such an incredible career -
  what's the secret to your success?"
- Committee-question stacking: "Given the current landscape of X,
  and considering Y, and also Z, how would you..." One question.
- "For our listeners who might not know..." (this is Cowen's classic
  peeve - the guest is a peer, the listener should keep up).
- "In an ideal world..." (either the world you're describing is
  possible or it isn't; either way ask about that instead).
- Prediction demands the guest can't fulfil: "Where do you see [huge
  domain] in twenty years?" is only worth asking if the guest has a
  real forecasting record. Otherwise it's fluff.
- Overrated / underrated on things the guest has no view on. Skip
  the item if it doesn't have punch.

## Anti-patterns (delete on sight)

1. **The setup essay.** A three-sentence context paragraph before the
   question. Cut the essay; the question stands.
2. **The gotcha.** Cowen is direct but never trying to trap the guest.
   If a question sounds like a takedown, rewrite it as a genuine ask.
3. **The both-sides.** "Some people say X, others say Y - what do
   you think?" Just ask what they think. Or, better, name a specific
   proponent of X and a specific proponent of Y.
4. **The soft biographical.** "What was your favorite subject in
   school?" - too generic. Better: "You've said in [source] that [X]
   was formative - can you unpack that?" or "Your mother was a
   [profession] - what did you learn from watching her work?"
5. **Time-of-recording questions.** "What do you think about
   [current news item]?" ages badly and rarely reveals the guest's
   underlying model. Ask about the underlying phenomenon, not the
   news cycle.
6. **The mystery question.** A cute setup that doesn't land in a
   clear ask. If the guest could reasonably respond "sorry, what's
   the question?", rewrite.
7. **Explaining the reference.** If you name a book / paper /
   thinker, don't then explain who they are to the guest. Assume
   they know. If they don't, that's the audience's problem, not the
   guest's.

## Self-edit checklist

Before handing the question set back:

- [ ] Every question cites something specific (a book / paper /
      argument / biographical fact / location / era).
- [ ] At least one steelman-a-view-you-oppose question.
- [ ] At least one "why might you be wrong" question.
- [ ] Personal / biographical questions are present and specific,
      not generic "tell me about yourself" fluff.
- [ ] Overrated / underrated segment items are tuned to this guest.
- [ ] No warm-up softballs. First question is substantive.
- [ ] No committee questions - one ask per bullet.
- [ ] No "for our listeners" scaffolding.
- [ ] No prediction demands unless the guest has a forecasting
      record.
- [ ] Cross-domain jumps included - not everything is on the guest's
      core beat.
- [ ] A production function question is in there (one, not five).
- [ ] The close is a real question, not "what's next for you".
