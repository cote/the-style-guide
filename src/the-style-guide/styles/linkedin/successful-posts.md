# Successful LinkedIn posts

A positive / how-to style for writing LinkedIn posts that perform on
the platform's actual engagement metrics. Load this when the user
asks for a LinkedIn post that will get reach, comments, reactions, or
reposts.

Typical triggers:

- "Write me a LinkedIn post about..."
- "Help me write a LinkedIn post that will actually perform."
- "How do I get engagement on this LinkedIn post?"
- "Turn this into a LinkedIn post."
- "Draft a LinkedIn post for [announcement / talk / news]."
- "Make this LinkedIn post get more reach."

**Deliberately not covered here**: taste, whether the post "sounds
like LinkedIn", or whether the writer will feel embarrassed by it.
Those questions belong in `no-linkedin-talk.md` alongside this file.
The goal here is *effectiveness at the platform's game*. Load the
anti-cliché style separately if the user wants both.

## Ground the strategy in the evidence

Grounded in Usera, Cox & Walker (2026), which coded ~1,000 individual
users' posts into five categories and tested engagement differences
statistically. Claims that come directly from that paper are marked
**[paper]**; extrapolations are marked **[editorial]**. Full
citation at the bottom.

The paper's five post categories:

- **Interpersonal** - posts about other people (praise, thanks, "great to meet you at").
- **Personal** - posts about the poster (career updates, life events).
- **Business** - self- or company-promotional (job openings, product news, sales).
- **Expertise** - insight / thought-leadership content, often link-heavy.
- **Observances** - holidays, awareness days, causes.

## The engagement hierarchy

By metric **[paper]**:

| Goal | Best categories | Worst |
|------|-----------------|-------|
| Reactions | Interpersonal, Observances | Expertise |
| Comments | Interpersonal, Personal | Business, Expertise |
| Reposts | Business, Observances | Interpersonal, Personal |

Three headlines:

1. **Posts about other people (Interpersonal) win visible engagement.** Appreciation, congratulations, and "great to meet you" posts won more head-to-head comparisons for reactions and comments than any other category. **[paper]**
2. **Posts about causes and observances punch far above their weight.** Only 5.45% of the sampled posts, but undefeated in reactions and dominant in reposts. **[paper]**
3. **Expertise posts underperform.** Second most frequent category (~19% of posts), but won only one of seven head-to-head comparisons. Thought-leadership is oversupplied and under-rewarded on visible engagement. **[paper]**

## The scarcity arbitrage

The two best-performing categories (Interpersonal + Observances) are
also the two least-posted, together about 15% of the feed. The
most-posted category (Business, ~43%) performs poorly on reactions
and comments. **[paper]**

The feed is saturated with self-promotion and starved of
other-promotion. **Post what's scarce.** **[editorial]**

## Pick the post type from your goal

- **Want reach (reposts)?** Business content - job openings, product news, concrete announcements - is what people find easiest to re-share, alongside Observances. **[paper]**
- **Want conversation (comments)?** Interpersonal and Personal. Career updates and posts naming real people draw replies. **[paper]**
- **Want visibility (reactions)?** Interpersonal and Observances. **[paper]**
- **Want people to actually read your article / watch your talk?** Expertise posts may win on click-through, which the study could not measure. Low reactions on a link post may mean readers left the platform to consume the linked thing, which is the whole point. **[paper]**

Don't let the reaction count fool you either direction. Optimize for
the metric that serves the actual goal.

## Mechanics that move the numbers

- **Tag the people you name.** The authors' leading hypothesis for why Interpersonal posts dominate: tagging triggers notifications, which mechanically extends reach. **[paper]** Don't tag people irrelevantly - that's engagement-bait and reads as such. **[editorial]**
- **Keep the payload in the post.** External links may pull readers off-platform before they react or comment - the paper's hypothesis for why link-heavy Expertise posts underperform. Write the post so it delivers value even if nobody clicks; the link is a footnote, not the point. **[paper]**
- **Match register to goal.** From the paper's cited literature: infographic-style posts outperformed academic and public-information styles (Huang et al., 2019), and posts perceived as "exciting" drew likes while "competent" ones drew clicks (Mora Cortez & Ghosh Dastidar, 2022). Warm and vivid gets visible engagement; authoritative gets click-throughs. **[paper]**
- **The bar is low.** 34% of sampled users had never posted; the average post was 499 days old; 43% of "posts" were actually reposts. Merely writing original posts with any regularity puts you in a small minority. **[paper]**

## Engagement playbook

1. **Make someone else the subject.** The single strongest pattern in the data: posts praising, thanking, or acknowledging a specific named person outperform posts about yourself. **[paper]** Specificity is the active ingredient - "Gerry rebuilt our CI pipeline over a weekend and cut build times 60%" beats "shoutout to my amazing team." **[editorial]**
2. **Tag the people you name.** Mechanical reach boost. **[paper]** Only tag when it's genuinely relevant. **[editorial]**
3. **Convert self-promotion into other-promotion.** Instead of "I gave a talk at DevNexus", write about the three people whose questions changed how you think about the topic. Same news, higher-performing category. **[editorial]**
4. **Hang timely posts on observances sparingly and sincerely.** Observance posts overperform **[paper]**, but a hollow "Happy International Women's Day" from someone with no track record on the subject reads as opportunistic. Only post observances you have standing to post. **[editorial]**
5. **Put the insight in the post, not behind the link.** Write the post so it delivers value even if nobody clicks. Link goes at the end or in a comment. **[paper-derived]**
6. **If reach is the goal, make the post effortlessly re-shareable.** Job postings and concrete announcements get reposted because re-sharing them is a low-cost favor. Give people a reason and a frictionless way to pass it along. **[paper]**
7. **One post, one intention.** The researchers had to build precedence rules because 74 posts couldn't decide what they were - advice that turns into a sales pitch, gratitude that's secretly a career announcement. **[paper]** Readers detect the bait-and-switch too. If it's an ad, be an ad. **[editorial]**

## Recipe: high-performing patterns by post type

### Interpersonal (praise / thanks / recognition)

- Named person as the subject in the first sentence.
- One specific, checkable thing they did. Numbers if you have them.
- The concrete effect on you, your team, or the work.
- Tag them.
- Close on them, not on you.

Skip: generic "amazing team" praise, group thank-yous with a
hashtag, "so grateful to be surrounded by so much talent" openers.

### Observance (holiday / awareness day / cause)

- Only post one you have standing to post. A record of work on the
  subject, a personal connection, or a specific action you took today.
- Lead with a concrete detail from that record or action, not with
  the day's name.
- Include one concrete ask if the goal is impact (donate here, hire
  from this pool, read this specific report).
- Short. Not a manifesto.

Skip: stock inspirational quotes, pastel graphics, generic
"celebrating [group] today" posts with no substance behind them.

### Business (job opening / product news / announcement)

- Facts first: what, when, where, how to act on it. Reposts happen
  because sharing is a low-cost favor - make it easy.
- Include one line about *why this matters to the person reading*,
  not just to you.
- Tag one person if there's a natural attribution (the hiring
  manager, the launch lead), not five for reach.
- Explicit CTA is fine here - the whole point of the post is a
  transaction.

Skip: "excited to share" openers, corporate cheerleading, framing
the announcement as a personal journey.

### Personal (career update / life event)

- The news itself as the first line. "Starting at X next Monday."
  "Finished the certification." "Home from three weeks in Y."
- One or two sentences of specific context. What the move is about,
  what you learned, what you're doing next.
- Optionally: one named person who helped, tagged.

Skip: "I am beyond thrilled and humbled to announce...", the
long-run-up-to-good-news structure, gratitude-as-brag ("I've been
so blessed with the opportunities...").

### Expertise (insight / analysis / thought leadership)

Underperforms on visible engagement per the paper, but has real
click-through value the paper couldn't measure. If you're posting
expertise:

- Put the actual insight in the post body. Assume nobody clicks the
  link - the post should be worth reading standalone.
- One specific claim, one specific piece of evidence, one specific
  implication. Not a mini-essay.
- The link is a citation, not a hook. Put it at the end or in a
  comment.
- If you want reactions, name a specific person's work you're
  building on and tag them (converts Expertise into partial
  Interpersonal).
- Short. Long insight posts get worse engagement than short ones
  because readers scroll past on mobile.

Skip: "Here's a thread on..." (LinkedIn isn't Twitter), inflated
significance claims, gated "DM me for the full report" plays.

## Pre-publish checklist

1. Is the subject of this post someone other than me? If not, is
   there a version where it could be?
2. Have I tagged the people I named? Only the relevant ones?
3. Does the post deliver its value without anyone clicking the link?
4. Am I optimizing for the metric that actually serves my goal
   (reactions vs. comments vs. reposts vs. clicks)?
5. One post, one intention - ad, thanks, insight, or announcement,
   not all four?
6. Would the opener survive being read aloud in a normal human
   voice? (This is where a separate anti-cliché pass helps -
   see `no-linkedin-talk.md`.)

## Caveats (paper limitations)

The underlying study is observational and descriptive:

- No controls for follower count, poster credibility, job type, or post age.
- Single-coded posts.
- Sample skewed to Dallas-Fort Worth users.
- No click-through data measured.

**[paper]** "Interpersonal posts got more engagement in this sample"
does not strictly license "Interpersonal posts will get *you* more
engagement." Treat this guide as informed heuristics, not physics.
