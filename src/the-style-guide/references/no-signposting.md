# No signposting, no meta-talk

The most common AI-writing tic Coté wants stripped: telling the reader
what you're about to say is important, instead of just saying it. Cute
hat on top of the actual sentence. Strip it.

## The pattern

Signposting reads like a tour guide announcing the next exhibit.
Meta-talk reads like the writer narrating their own writing. Both
produce sentences that sit *adjacent* to the substance rather than
carrying it.

The give-away phrasings:

- "is the most honest sentence in the literature"
- "is the actual finding, and the one nobody wants to write down"
- "is the thing worth sitting with"
- "is what I want to focus on"
- "is what this essay is really about"
- "what I find striking is..."
- "the thing that matters here is..."
- "the more interesting question is the one I have been asking myself"
- "I want to call out..."
- "it's worth noting that..."

If a sentence's job is to *frame* the next sentence as important,
delete it and let the next sentence do its job.

## Examples

### "The most honest sentence in the literature"

**Before:**

> The procurement officer's "it's not an issue" is the most honest sentence in the literature. We have been talking about this for thirty years.

**After:**

> We have been talking about this for thirty years.

The rest of the paragraph makes the connection without anyone needing
to label the quote "honest." Saying it out loud weakens it.

### "That is the actual finding, and the one nobody wants to write down"

**Before:**

> That is the actual finding, and the one nobody wants to write down: we know what's wrong, we know what would work, and we don't do it because nothing in the system requires anyone to.

**After:**

> We know what's wrong, we know what would work, and we don't do it because nothing in the system requires anyone to.

The setup is a hat that says "important sentence incoming." Pull the
hat off; the sentence stands.

### Author-narration of the writing process

**Before:**

> Halfway through the conversation, I found myself working out, in real time, a frame that I had not quite articulated before:
>
> > Sometimes governance and security is really annoying...

**After:**

> On the call I tried out a frame I'd been circling:
>
> > Sometimes governance and security is really annoying...

The "in real time" and "had not quite articulated before" are
author-narration. The reader doesn't need the live-blog.

### "The most important sentence in the recording"

**Before:**

> One speaker closed with what I think is the most important sentence in the recording, on what made the engagement actually work:

**After:**

> One speaker ended on what made the engagement work:

If a quote is doing the heavy lifting, don't tell the reader it's heavy.

### "That last sentence is the one the literature keeps gesturing at"

**Before:**

> [quote]
>
> That last sentence is the one the institutional literature keeps gesturing at and never quite saying out loud. The technical pattern matters...

**After:**

> [quote]
>
> The technical pattern matters...

Cut the meta-sentence entirely. The substantive paragraph that follows
tells the reader what to take from the quote without announcing that
it's going to.

### "What's striking about X is Y"

**Before:**

> What's striking about reading the Fraser/Ho piece next to Kroger and Viray's *Software Factory 2.0* white paper, five years later, is how completely they predict each other.

**After:**

> The Fraser/Ho piece predicts Kroger and Viray's *Software Factory 2.0* white paper five years before it was written.

"What's striking about X is Y" is the most common version of this tic.
Just say Y.

### "The most thoughtful diagnoses converge to a remarkable degree"

**Before:**

> The most thoughtful diagnoses of the problem converge to a remarkable degree. Researchers, practitioners, vendors, and academics, working in different countries and across different decades, keep arriving at the same short list.

**After:**

> Researchers, practitioners, vendors, and academics keep arriving at the same short list - across countries, across decades.

"Converge to a remarkable degree" signposts the next sentence. The new
sentence demonstrates convergence directly.

### "The more interesting question is the one I have been asking myself"

**Before:**

> Given that the diagnosis is convergent and largely uncontested, the more interesting question is the one I have been asking myself: why don't we get over it?

**After:**

> The diagnosis is convergent and largely uncontested. Why don't we get over it?

### "The partial answers are themselves illuminating"

**Before:**

> The literature contains a few partial answers, and the partial answers are themselves illuminating because they don't all agree.

**After:**

> Four partial answers show up in the literature. They don't all agree.

The "are themselves illuminating because" is a tour guide pointing at
the exhibit. Two short declarative sentences carry the same content
with no commentary.

### "Does not look like a recommendation in the conventional sense"

**Before:**

> One practitioner put the same finding in one sentence: you don't get to production without good relationships. That observation does not look like a recommendation in the conventional sense because it does not generalize cleanly into a procurement clause or a policy document. It is, however, what every successful engagement seems to require, public or private.

**After:**

> One practitioner put the same finding in one sentence: you don't get to production without good relationships. It doesn't generalize cleanly into a procurement clause, but it's what every successful engagement requires.

### Even mild forms

**Before:**

> What is striking about the prescriptions, taken together, is that they are also convergent.

**After:**

> The prescriptions are also convergent.

### Opening-level meta-talk

**Before:**

> That sentence sits at the centre of every honest conversation about government software, and it captures, more cleanly than any consultant's deck, the shape of the problem we keep failing to fix.

**After:**

> That captures, more cleanly than any consultant's deck, the shape of the problem.

The "sits at the centre of every honest conversation" half is doing
pure declaration-of-importance work. The remaining clause still lands.

## Self-edit checklist

Scan a draft for these and rewrite:

1. **"is the most ___ sentence/quote/finding in the ___"** - delete the whole frame.
2. **"What's striking about ___ is ___"** - cut the frame, just state what's striking.
3. **"The thing that matters here is ___"** / **"the thing worth sitting with"** - cut.
4. **"That observation/sentence/finding is the one that ___"** - cut the meta-sentence.
5. **"Given that ___, the more interesting question is ___"** - split into two short sentences. State the given, ask the question.
6. **"The literature contains/shows/reveals ___"** - if you can replace with a direct fact, do.
7. **"What I find ___ is ___"** / **"What I want to focus on is ___"** - delete the I-am-talking-now scaffold.
8. **"It is worth noting that ___"** - delete.
9. **"In real time"** / **"working out"** / **"I had not quite articulated before"** - author-narration. Compress or cut.
10. **"That is the actual finding, and the one nobody wants to write down"** (and variants: "the answer nobody wants to write", "the part nobody mentions") - cut the frame, let the finding stand naked.

## Why it matters

These phrasings cost the writing two things at once. They sound like AI
throat-clearing - the giveaway of a draft that was generated rather
than thought. And they undercut the actual claim by setting up an
expectation the next sentence rarely lives up to ("this is the most
important sentence in the recording" promises something the quote can
rarely deliver on).

Direct statement reads like someone who knows what they think. Framed
statement reads like someone narrating their own credibility.

When in doubt: delete the framing sentence and re-read. If the
paragraph still works, the frame was decoration.
